\autoref{ch:bg:scalability} reviewed the main ways to scale blockchains and ended each review on what is still missing.
This short chapter closes Part~I by gathering those gaps and turning them into the research questions of this thesis. \mat{CHECK: RQ1--RQ3 are stated in \autoref{sec:bg:challenges}; keep their wording identical to the Introduction once it is written.}

\autoref{sec:bg:challenges} groups the limitations into three open challenges, one for each of the following parts.
The first is to check, on real data, the workload assumptions that many designs rely on.
The second is to place state across shards in a way that cuts cross-shard transactions while staying small, verifiable and resistant to manipulation.
The third is to verify the availability of layer-2 data by sampling, within the timing of Ethereum's consensus.
\autoref{sec:bg:requirements} sets out what the solutions to the last two must share, whether they shard computation on layer~1 or data for layer~2.
They must be verifiable by any node without a trusted party, yet flexible enough for large and changing systems, and they must stay decentralized and practical to deploy.
Finally, \autoref{sec:bg:positioning} places the contributions relative to each other and gives the roadmap for Parts~II to~IV.
SOAPS and PANDAS address different bottlenecks and complement each other.

\section{Open Challenges}\label{sec:bg:challenges}

% \begin{itemize}
% 	\item C1: designs assume locality, rarely checked on real, recent data (Part II). Question: solid assumptions about the near future from historical data?
% 	\item C2: state placement that cuts cross-shard transactions with small metadata, verifiable, not biasable; fits existing consensus, no trusted coordinator (Part III).
% 	\item C3: L2 data availability checked by sampling within consensus timing over an open P2P network; 4\,s, tens of thousands of nodes, faults, no new consensus step (Part IV).
% \end{itemize}

The limitations identified in \autoref{ch:bg:scalability} reduce to three open challenges.
Each motivates one research question, and one part of this thesis.

\paragraph{C1: Knowing the workload.}
Many of the techniques reviewed in \autoref{sec:bg:workload} and \autoref{sec:bg:sharding} rely, explicitly or not, on properties of the transaction workload.
Parallel execution and sharding assume that concurrent transactions mostly access disjoint state.
Light nodes, stateless clients and snapshot-based bootstrap assume that recently used state is reused.
Placement optimizations go further and assume that past interactions between accounts predict future ones.
These assumptions are rarely measured on a real, large and recent workload, \mat{CHECK: Part~II currently uses pre-Merge data; either update it with recent data (plan TODO) or drop ``recent'' here.} and designs are often evaluated on synthetic workloads or limited traces. \mat{CHECK: soften or support ``rarely measured'' and ``often evaluated on synthetic workloads or limited traces'' with a few examples from the related work.}
Whether they hold, for which accounts, and whether they remain stable over time, determines how much these designs can actually gain.
This leads to our first research question.

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{RQ1.} Can we derive solid assumptions on the near-future workload of a blockchain from its historical transaction data?
\end{quote}

\paragraph{C2: Placing state safely.}
State sharding splits execution and state across shards, but hash-based placement makes nearly every Ethereum transaction cross-shard (\autoref{sec:bg:sharding:state}).
Placement optimizations reduce this share, but assign every account explicitly, which is too much metadata for every node to keep at Ethereum's scale.
Most of them also leave open who computes the placement and how others can check it (\autoref{sec:bg:sharding:limitations}).
In an open blockchain, a placement that a single party decides without verification invites selfish optimization, and can even help an attacker gather target accounts in a shard it controls.
The designs that do make placement verifiable have every node or a whole committee redo the optimization.
What is needed is a placement that reduces cross-shard transactions with bounded metadata, that any node can verify, and that fits into the existing consensus and its incentives without a trusted coordinator.

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{RQ2.} How can state be placed in an account-based sharded blockchain to reduce cross-shard transactions, without centralizing placement decisions or exposing them to selfish optimization?
\end{quote}

\paragraph{C3: Checking data availability in time.}
Rollups move the pressure onto layer~1, which must make their data available (\autoref{sec:bg:l2}).
Broadcasting all of it to every node does not scale beyond a few blobs per block, and data availability sampling lets each node check availability while downloading only a small part.
In Ethereum, however, sampling must complete within the first 4 seconds of a slot, so that attesters can vote on the block and on the availability of its data at the same time.
It must work over an open peer-to-peer network of thousands to tens of thousands of nodes, some of them faulty, unresponsive or with an incomplete view of the network, and without adding a step to consensus.
Existing proposals either do not scale to the target data volume, weaken the guarantees of sampling, or leave the sampling process unspecified (\autoref{sec:bg:l2:limitations}).

\begin{quote}
	\textbf{RQ3.} How can layer-2 data be disseminated and sampled within Ethereum's consensus time bounds, in a fully decentralized, peer-to-peer way?
\end{quote}

\section{Common Requirements for L1 and L2 Sharding}\label{sec:bg:requirements}

% \begin{itemize}
% 	\item Verifiable: any node can recompute who is assigned what; same trick as Ethereum committees.
% 	\item Flexible: adapt in large, changing systems; between fully deterministic and fully ad hoc.
% 	\item Decentralized: no heavy new consensus step, low cost per node, open participation.
% 	\item Practical: real Ethereum workloads and timing, compatible with existing protocols.
% \end{itemize}

Challenges C2 and C3 concern different resources: execution and state on layer~1, and the data that layer-2 protocols publish on it.
Both are forms of sharding, however, since both make each node responsible for only part of the system.
They share a central difficulty: deciding \emph{who is responsible for what}, accounts for shards or data for nodes, in an open system where some participants are adversarial.
We derive four requirements that our solutions to both challenges must meet.

\paragraph{Verifiable.}
Any node must be able to recompute or check, on its own, who is assigned what, without trusting a third party.
Ethereum already solves this problem for its committees.
The assignment of validators is a deterministic function of public randomness and public state, so any node can recompute it and reject a vote from the wrong committee (\autoref{sec:bg:eth-pos:committees}).
Our designs reuse this principle.
SOAPS elects, from the beacon chain's randomness, the node that computes the placement and those that check it.
PANDAS derives from the epoch seed the part of the data that each node keeps.

\paragraph{Flexible.}
Determinism alone is not enough.
A fully static assignment, such as hash-based placement, cannot follow the workload, and a fixed communication plan cannot cope with nodes that fail or that see the network differently.
A fully ad hoc approach, on the other hand, cannot be checked.
Our designs sit in between.
They fix the part of the decision that must be verifiable, and leave a bounded part free to adapt.
In SOAPS, a limited set of explicit placement rules is recomputed periodically from the workload.
In PANDAS, nodes adapt how they fetch data to the conditions they observe.

\paragraph{Decentralized.}
The solutions must not concentrate power or cost.
They must add no heavy consensus step, such as running a BFT agreement on every placement update, and keep the cost of an ordinary node within the hardware and bandwidth recommended for Ethereum~\cite{eip7870}.
Anyone must remain able to participate.

\paragraph{Practical.}
The solutions must be grounded in how Ethereum actually works.
They should be evaluated on real Ethereum workloads and under its consensus timing, and remain compatible with the existing protocols, rather than assume a blockchain designed from scratch.

\autoref{fig:bg:positioning} places the contributions of this thesis along two axes: the layer concerned, and whether what is sharded is computation or data.

SOAPS (Part~III) shards computation on layer~1.
It makes state sharding practical by placing explicitly only a bounded set of the accounts that cause the most cross-shard transactions, and by integrating the computation and verification of this placement into consensus.
Its benefit depends on the workload, and it builds directly on Part~II, which identifies the small set of accounts responsible for most of the loss of spatial locality.

PANDAS (Part~IV) shards data.
It removes the bandwidth bottleneck of data availability, so that layer~1 can host far more layer-2 data, while each node handles only a small share of it.
It depends very little on the workload, since erasure-coded cells carry no semantic locality.
Its difficulty lies instead in the network: latency, failures, and nodes with inconsistent views, under a hard deadline.

The two contributions complement each other rather than compete.
A sharded layer~1 still has to host the data of the rollups built on it, and the data of each shard could itself be sampled.
Conversely, scaling data availability does not make execution on layer~1 cheaper.
The workload analysis of Part~II informs the first, and, beyond this thesis, could inform parallel execution in rollup sequencers.

The rest of this thesis follows this structure.
Part~II analyzes the locality of Ethereum transactions and answers RQ1~\cite{pigaglio2023exploring}.
Part~III presents SOAPS and answers RQ2. \mat{TODO: cite the SOAPS paper here (and in the Part~III row of \autoref{tab:bg:placement}) once it has a bib entry.}

The conclusion compares the two designs, summarizes the answers to the research questions, and discusses perspectives.
